Residential model here to stay ‘but needs rethink’

Fixing student accommodation key to tackling raft of other student issues, argues Hepi report

Leaving home to study will remain a central part of British higher education, but the way student accommodation is planned and paid for needs a fresh look, according to a new report from the Higher Education Policy Institute (Hepi).

The report, Moving Away? The past and future of student accommodation, was written by William Whyte, professor of social and architectural history at the University of Oxford, and commissioned by the UPP Foundation. It combines historical analysis with contemporary evidence and students’ own accounts of life in halls.

A lasting commitment to leaving home

Hepi argues that the expectation of moving away for university has proved remarkably durable. Despite big shifts in student numbers, diversity and finance, the report says the share of UK students who live at home, about 21 per cent, has barely changed in around 80 years. Across Europe, by comparison, around 36 per cent of students live with their parents.

The report contends that residence offers far more than a bed. Living alongside peers, it says, helps students make friends, grow in confidence and independence, and take part in sport and societies, benefits that it links to a greater chance of earning a good degree. It also suggests these advantages are hard to reproduce online, strengthening the case for the residential experience as artificial intelligence changes how students learn.

An affordability gap

Fixing accommodation, the report argues, is key to tackling a wider set of student problems. Maintenance support no longer covers the full cost of living for many students, and in some areas rents exceed the maximum support available. As a result, more students are taking paid work during term: according to the report, the proportion doing so rose from 35 per cent in 2015 to 68 per cent in 2025.

Hepi says long working hours eat into time for study and reduce students’ chances of top results, while also cutting them off from the social, health and community benefits that residential life is meant to provide.

Professor Whyte says there is currently “no real policy on accommodation” for most students who want to leave home to study, and calls for a serious debate about the role housing plays in university life and how it should be funded. The report argues that discussion has focused too narrowly on buildings and beds, and urges universities to rediscover the idea of the institution as a living community as well as a place of learning.

It closes with six practical recommendations for policymakers, universities and accommodation providers. The report comes as demand for places continues to grow: Ucas figures for the January 2026 deadline, cited by Hepi, showed applications from UK 18-year-olds up 4.8 per cent to 338,940.
