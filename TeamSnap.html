We Have a Window of Opportunity with Youth Soccer. Let’s Not Waste It.

By Peter Frintzilas

World Cup fever broke in July. The news cycle has moved on.

But what matters most in youth sports is not usually in the headlines. It is what happens on the field, on a Tuesday, in a town nobody is covering — and as the fall season winds down, the tournament’s fingerprint has not faded there at all.

Frintzilas argues the summer’s new fans only matter if that enthusiasm reaches kids who want to play, including those without a team, and that the whole industry shares that responsibility.

The momentum is real

With 30 million users to date, over 185 million scheduled events and two million daily app users, TeamSnap often sees shifts early, and he says this one showed up: August soccer participation hit a three-year high alongside the World Cup, and soccer rosters grew 18% in 2026.

The Sports & Fitness Industry Association also logged a 6.2% gain in outdoor soccer during the tournament’s first weeks. He cites MLS GO and MLS NEXT training reaching coaches through TeamSnap, and U.S. Soccer absorbing US Youth Soccer, with 2.7 million players and 54 state associations, into its U.S. Soccer Pathways Strategy. Despite the USMNT’s round-of-16 exit, he calls the moment ripe.

When momentum is not enough

Booms after the 1994 World Cup and 1999 Women’s World Cup faded, he writes, partly as costs rose.

A main sport now costs families over $1,000 per child a year, almost 50% more than five years ago, and over two-thirds of parents have considered pulling their kids out, mainly over cost.

He blames long underinvestment in local providers such as Little Leagues, YMCAs, Boys & Girls Clubs and parks departments. When they fade, wealthier families buy into private clubs and others drop out. Citing The Good Sports Cost of Play Index, where 67% of parents report fewer affordable options, he says COVID-era closures removed many families’ only way in.

Growing the game means investing where it starts

For him, access is the key question, and businesses, leagues, governing bodies and policymakers all help decide it.

TeamSnap supports the STRONG Kids Act, which would fund grassroots programs from existing sports-wagering excise tax revenue and target the communities with the fewest options, as part of a six-year effort led by the FundPlay Foundation.

He expects brands to spend big around Brazil in 2027, Los Angeles in 2028 and the 2031 World Cup, but urges them to lower fees, sponsor leagues and back coaches rather than just appear for the big event. TeamSnap says it has invested more than $20 million in youth sports.

The time is now

The real measure, he suggests, is how many kids still have a team when the World Cup is back on the continent in 2031. Youth soccer is ready, he concludes, but the window is roughly five years, and the industry owes it to the coaches, volunteers and parents to make sure a team is waiting for every child.
